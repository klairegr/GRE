## Garden Description
<!-- This is the main text describing the garden -->
This house was south of the ancient city, 250 meters outside the ramparts but north of the circus.  In 1977, during salvage excavations before the construction of  a building on the rue du 11 Novembre to be called “Les Nymphéas” (The Waterlilies), about 700 square meters were explored.  To the west, the insula was bordered by a street next to the river.  The vestiges belonged to several *domus* which went through three principal phases.  

<!-- According to A. Pelletier, in the first phase (NAR 21.2.2) the remains could belong to just one house, but with good reason A. Bouet believes the south part to be a different house, organized around a central corridor. Indeed, a 31-meter long wall seems to completely separate the two structures.  The north house had a peristyle [1] of which three galleries are known. The center was entirely occupied by a rectangular pool [2] (4.50 x 2.85 m) bordered by a high wall that could have had windows.  According to the excavator, there was a second pool, only slightly smaller (4.50 x 2.45 m) enclosed by the first. In our opinion, the same structure might well be interpreted as a raised border of the pool.  The floors of the house were all covered with *opus signinum* save in the east gallery of the peristyle, where the floor was pounded earth. 

Paintings decorated the south wall. Up to a height of about one meter, they survived in place for a length of 7.68 meters.  Above an ochre yellow base 25 centimeters high was a dark band about of 60 centimeters width divided into panels by narrow, white lines.  In the panels, a total of six all-white stilts (wading birds) alternate with tufts of foliage and flowers, perhaps iris.  Fragmentary remains of painted plaster from this or another wing of the peristyle show, above the same composition at the bottom, a middle zone partitioned by candelabras, some with a feminine figure or winged genie at the top.  

To judge from sample pits, the first phase of construction began in the Augustan age. The 
paintings of the stilts were originally thought to have been done in the Tiberian-Claudian age, (30 to 40 A.D.) in surroundings of the Pompeian third style.  New comparisons, especially of the details of the candelabra, would place them more probably in the fourth style and thus in the middle of the century. (Communication from A. Barbet.)-->
In a second phase, the north house was reworked.  Its peristyle was enlarged to 11 x 8.5 meters for the known parts, by pushing back the the north and west sides. A channel pool or rill 70 centimeters wide and 60 centimeters deep, known along the south and east sides, was laid along the porticoes.  In the south house, the rooms were re-arranged around a large courtyard (9bis).  This transformation is placed by A. Pelletier in the decade of the 70's.In a third phase (NAR 21.2.5), the north house seems almost unchanged, keeps its passageways in *opus signinum*, but adds an *emblema* of fish and duck in one of the rooms opening onto the peristyle. The south house, on the other hand, was profoundly altered.  It seems to have been raised some fifty centimeters.  The previous court [9bis] had become definite a garden [9ter] about six meters on a side. It is surrounded on three sides by galleries two meters wide and paved with stone.  Against the west gallery, in front of the reception room, was placed a rectangular pool (4.4 x1.0 m, 40 cm deep). Drainage from the northwest corner connected to a sewer which led to the street on the west, river side.  The whole house, trapezoidal in plan, covered 391 square meters. 
A painting of a stilt walking on a red ochre ground amid plants was found on the south wall of the garden. This type of décor is usual on the internal walls of a peristyle.  The painting had a height of at least 72.5 centimeters, but the passageways around [9ter] are not well known.  This painting encroaches on the space of room [8], which kept its simple preexistent decor. 
A. Pelletier tends to place the third phase between at the end of the first or beginning of the second century, but Alix Barbet assigns the painting of the stilt on red background to the epoch of Vespasian.
The site was apparently abandoned at the end of the second century.
